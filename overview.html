Literature Review

Overview of Research

Social media is an extremely complex topic, as our research shows. It is difficult to classify social media as simply positive or negative, since it can be a large mix of both. Social media can be used as a tool for connection, especially among college-age students. It can be used to connect this age group with long-distance friends and family, and help them feel less isolated. Social media can also help people find online communities of people similar to them, making them feel like they belong.

However, social media can also have several negative effects on users in this age group. Social media usage in young adults has been linked to increases in anxiety and depression, as well as self-image issues. Social media causes comparison between young adults, and posting can be viewed as a sort of competition. College-aged kids feel pressure to present themselves a certain way, and feel behind or excluded when it feels like other people are having more fun than they are. Because of this, social media is not just good or bad; it is extremely complex.

Negative Impacts

Although social media usage is high among college students, it is consistently linked to negative mental health outcomes, especially when use is frequent, passive, or comparison-driven. Multiple studies demonstrate a correlation between high social media use and increased anxiety and depression. Fruehwirth et al. (2024) found that increased social media use was associated with worsening mental health outcomes over time, particularly after the pandemic. These findings suggest that the negative effects of social media may intensify as usage becomes more habitual. Similarly, Fatima et al. (2025) found that excessive social media use is associated with higher levels of anxiety and depression, specifically among college students. This study also highlights how academic pressure, combined with social media use, can further worsen these outcomes, making college students especially vulnerable.

In addition, the way individuals engage with social media plays a critical role. Wang et al. (2025) found that passive scrolling is more strongly associated with depression than active engagement, such as posting or messaging. Passive use often leads to increased comparison, reduced interaction, and greater feelings of isolation. This reinforces the idea that it is not just the amount of time spent on social media, but how it is used that influences mental health outcomes.

Social media also encourages constant comparison to idealized, curated versions of others’ lives. Because users tend to share only the most positive aspects of their experiences, viewers are exposed to unrealistic standards. Anto et al. (2023) identified social comparison and fear of missing out (FOMO) as key drivers of anxiety, noting that students frequently compare their lifestyles, appearance, and success to others online. This is further supported by the U.S. Surgeon General (2023) advisory, which states that social media can contribute to body image issues and identity concerns. These findings suggest that it is not just social media usage itself, but also how users interpret what they see, that impacts mental health.

Another negative effect is sleep disruption, which can further impact mental health. A systematic review by Ahmed et al. (2024) found that social media use is associated with poor sleep quality. This is significant because poor sleep is directly linked to increased anxiety, depression, and emotional instability, suggesting that social media may contribute to mental health challenges both directly and indirectly.

Social media is also highly habitual and difficult to regulate. The Healthy Minds Network (2024) report highlights rising levels of anxiety and stress among college students, with social media identified as a contributing factor. Many students use social media multiple times a day, which can lead to compulsive checking behaviors. Over time, this repeated exposure may compound negative mental health effects and reinforce patterns of stress and comparison.

Positive Impacts

Although a lot of our research highlights the downside of social media usage in young adults, there are several upsides that we wanted to highlight. Social media has also been proven to have positive effects on mental health. Especially for students away at college, social media can be used as a tool to stay in close connection with friends, family, and loved ones. This can be used to reduce feelings of isolation and sadness. There are also several online chat groups based on different interests, where students can feel understood and supported.

Social media has also been used to spread awareness about mental health and access to mental health resources. This also decreases feelings of isolation and has destigmatized mental health for those struggling. Social media has been used in many ways to portray positive messages and act as a safe space for emotional expression. This has helped some users feel less alone while they struggle.

Impact of Usage Patterns

As mentioned briefly earlier, not all social media use has the same impact. The effects depend not only on the amount of time spent, but also on how individuals engage with these platforms. Wang et al. (2025) conducted a study comparing passive versus active social media use. Passive use refers to scrolling and consuming content, while active use is defined as posting, messaging, or interacting with others. The study found that passive use is more strongly associated with depression. This may be because passive engagement often leads to increased comparison, reduced interaction, and a sense of disconnection, as users observe others’ lives rather than actively participating in their own.

Additionally, passive scrolling tends to be more mindless and less intentional. It often occurs during periods of boredom or procrastination, making it easy for individuals to lose track of time. As a result, users may spend extended periods scrolling, which can lead to feelings of guilt and dissatisfaction afterward. This prolonged exposure also increases contact with curated content and idealized lifestyles, making it easier for users to compare themselves to others and experience negative emotions.

Ultimately, these findings suggest that it is not solely how long individuals spend on social media, but how they experience it. The quality of engagement plays a more significant role than the quantity of time spent.

Gaps In Research

While the research we have provides important insight into the topics of social media and mental health, it is important to acknowledge the gaps in this research. Many of the studies we have focus broadly on young adults, without specifically focusing on college students. College students may experience unique academic and social pressures compared to young adults as a whole, making this a significant gap.

Also, a lot of the research we have shows a correlation between social media use and mental health, but doesn’t point to exact causation. Many of the studies also group all social media platforms together, rather than distinguishing the different effects that the different platforms have on users. There could be significant differences between the impacts that different types of social media have, such as TikTok, Instagram, and Snapchat.

Lastly, there is not a lot of real-world data that shows how much students interact with social media daily. These gaps show the need for more in-depth and targeted research, which is why we conducted the survey we included in the project.

Our Project's Alignment

Currently, there are growing concerns about college students’ relationships with social media and its impact on their well-being. While existing research provides valuable insight, it is often limited in scope. Many studies lack real-time perspectives or are broad in focus, rather than specifically examining college students.

To address this gap, our project incorporates primary data collected through a survey of current college students. This allows us to capture real responses and observe current behaviors in a technology-driven environment. The data reflects students’ actual habits and provides direct insight into how frequently and in what ways they engage with social media in their daily lives.

Our findings align with existing research and help reinforce patterns identified in prior studies. At the same time, they offer a more concrete and relatable perspective by illustrating how these patterns appear in real student experiences. In this way, our project helps bridge the gap between research and reality.

Additionally, our project focuses specifically on college students, a population that faces unique stressors such as academic pressure, social expectations, and increased independence. These factors make them particularly susceptible to the effects of social media. Overall, our project adds depth and relevance to existing research, making it more applicable to the lived experiences of college students today.
